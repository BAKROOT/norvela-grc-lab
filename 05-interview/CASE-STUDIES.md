# Timed Case Studies

Six cases in the formats real GRC interviews use. Do them under the stated
time limit with a timer running. Time pressure is the variable being tested —
you already know most of the content.

---

## Case 1 — The whiteboard risk assessment (20 min, verbal)

> *"A fintech has just acquired a small company. 40 people, one product,
> AWS, no security team. You have 20 minutes to tell me how you'd assess
> the risk of integrating them. Go."*

**What they are testing.** Whether you have a repeatable method or you
improvise. Whether you ask before you answer.

**Structure that works:**
1. **Clarify first (2 min).** What's the integration model — full absorption,
   or run separately? Are they in a regulated perimeter now? Does their
   product touch our customers' data? Timeline? *Asking these is scored.
   Candidates who launch straight into "first I'd do an asset inventory"
   lose points.*
2. **Scope (3 min).** Entities, systems, data, people, contracts.
3. **Method (5 min).** Crown jewels → threat scenarios → existing controls →
   inherent/residual → appetite comparison.
4. **The specifics that matter here (5 min).** Acquisitions have a
   characteristic risk profile: unknown credentials, unmanaged endpoints,
   personal accounts holding company data, undocumented vendor contracts
   that now bind you, and the acquired firm's own breach history which you
   inherit. Name those; they show you have thought about *acquisitions*, not
   just risk generally.
5. **Output and timeline (3 min).** What you'd deliver, when, and what you'd
   need from whom.
6. **What would stop the deal (2 min).** Have an answer. "Evidence of an
   unreported breach" and "a critical contract with no assignment clause"
   are good ones.

## Case 2 — The incident classification (30 min, written)

Use `01-scenario/10-INCIDENT.md` at the T+11h information state only.

> *"Here is what you know eleven hours in. Write the classification memo and
> the recommendation to the executive team. One page."*

**Scored on:** whether you classify against stated criteria rather than
instinct; whether you state what you don't know; whether you give a clear
recommendation rather than options; whether you handle the notification
clock explicitly.

**The trap.** Candidates wait for certainty. The regulatory clock does not.
A memo that says "we cannot yet confirm the start date; on the information
available this meets the major threshold on economic impact and criticality;
I recommend we notify on the assumption it is major and revise if facts
change" is the correct answer.

## Case 3 — The hostile stakeholder (15 min, role-play)

> *Interviewer plays Ivo Brandão, CTO. You are proposing a change freeze
> during the ISO audit window and a deployment approval gate.*

His lines, which you should expect:
- "We deploy 20 times a day. Your gate will halve our velocity."
- "The audit is a tax. It doesn't make us safer."
- "Name one incident a CAB would have prevented."
- "You've been here six weeks."

**What good looks like.** You do not win by citing the standard. You win by
finding the version of the control that costs him least: automated policy
gates in the pipeline rather than a committee; approval required only for
changes touching the payment path; evidence generated by the pipeline
itself so nobody fills in a form. Concede the velocity point honestly —
because he is right that a CAB would not have prevented NOR-2026-0908, and
saying so buys you the credibility to ask for what *would* have.

**Fail modes.** Appealing to authority ("the auditor requires it"). Getting
defensive. Agreeing to something you can't evidence.

## Case 4 — The document review (45 min, written)

> *"Here is a risk register. Critique it and tell us what you'd change."*

Use `03-workbooks/risk-register.xlsx`, `INHERITED-REGISTER`.

**Scored on** how many structural defects you find and whether you
prioritise. Finding twelve problems is good. Saying "the single change with
the most leverage is defining the scoring criteria, because without them
every other column is opinion" is what gets the offer.

## Case 5 — The board presentation (10 min presentation + 10 min questions)

> *"Present your quarterly risk report to the board. You have ten minutes."*

Build it from your Phase 6 work. Constraints: one page of slides, no more
than three. No control names. No framework names except where the board
needs them for a decision.

**Questions to expect:**
- "Are we secure?" *(Never yes or no. "Against the threats we've assessed,
  here's where we sit relative to the appetite you approved, and here are
  the two places we're outside it.")*
- "What keeps you up at night?" *(One specific thing, with a number.)*
- "Why should we spend this?" *(Loss avoided vs cost, from your FAIR work.)*
- "What happens if we don't?" *(Be concrete. Not "increased risk.")*
- Katarína Hoferová, the ex-regulator non-exec, will ask: *"What would our
  supervisor say if they saw this report?"* Have that answer ready.

## Case 6 — The take-home (4 hours, do it in 4)

> *"Perform a gap assessment of Norvela Pay against ISO 27001:2022 and
> produce a prioritised remediation roadmap with an indicative budget.
> Maximum 8 pages."*

This is the most common senior take-home format. Four hours means you cannot
assess 93 controls properly, so the real test is **how you triage** — and
whether you say so explicitly in the document.

**Structure that scores:**
1. Approach and limitations (½ page — state what you did not cover and why)
2. Scope and method (½ page)
3. Maturity summary by Annex A theme (1 page, with a visual)
4. Top 10 gaps with business impact (2 pages)
5. Roadmap: 30/90/180/365 days (2 pages)
6. Indicative budget and resourcing (1 page)
7. Assumptions and what you'd validate first (1 page)

**What separates good from excellent:** section 7. Everyone writes the
roadmap. Almost nobody writes the honest list of things they assumed and
would check before committing the budget. It is the section that signals
you have done this for real.
